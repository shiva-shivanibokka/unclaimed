\begin{abstract}
Language-model agents are increasingly asked to interview people and then make determinations that matter to them. In public benefits, the most harmful error is a \fq: telling a household it qualifies for help it will be denied. We study where the decisions of such an agent should live. Our system, built for Alexa+, lets the language model only phrase questions and explain results. Which question to ask next, when to stop, and what the household qualifies for are decided by code: a value-of-information policy that runs batched what-if simulations through an exact rules engine (PolicyEngine-US), over explicit known, unknown and declined state, so that no missing answer silently becomes a default. We evaluate against full-information reference answers on handwritten and generated households covering every eligibility cutoff, and in simulated conversations where a second model plays the person. Choosing questions by value of information reaches the full-information answer with fewer questions than asking everything; tracking unknowns removes nearly all \fq\ errors from partial interviews; and in simulated conversations on two model families, a model with the exact calculator as a tool still told a large share of people they qualify for a program they do not, in every case because of the facts it gave the calculator rather than by overriding it, while our design did so at most once in 38 conversations per model. Code, households and the E3 conversation transcripts are open source.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Each year many people eligible for public benefits never claim them: about one in five taxpayers eligible for the Earned Income Tax Credit \citep{irs2026eitcparticipation} and about one in eight people eligible for SNAP food assistance \citep{usda2025snapparticipation}. Screening tools exist, but they ask long fixed questionnaires. A voice agent can instead hold a conversation: ask a few questions, compute what the household may receive, and tell them how to apply. The risk is that a fluent model gives fluent wrong answers. Models misstate legal and tax rules with confidence \citep{dahl2024legalfictions,blairstanek2023statutory,bock2025taxcalcbench}, and incorrect chatbot suggestions measurably lower the accuracy of the caseworkers who rely on them \citep{gosciak2026socialservices}.

We argue that for an interviewing agent, \emph{where the decisions live} matters as much as how capable the model is. Tool use alone does not settle it: an agent with an exact calculator still decides which facts to collect and when it has enough, and a missing fact passed to a calculator becomes whatever the calculator's default is. We make three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A design} that separates conversation from decision. The model phrases and explains; code chooses each question by its value of information (computed exactly, by simulation), decides when to stop, and computes results. Every answer is known, unknown or declined; results that an unknown could still change say ``if \ldots'' rather than ``you qualify''.
  \item \textbf{An evaluation method} for interviewing agents: reference answers from full information; oracle interviews over handwritten households and over generated households placed at every program's eligibility cutoff; and simulated conversations with a model playing the person, all scored on \fq.
  \item \textbf{Evidence} from three experiments: question-selection policies (E1), the effect of tracking unknowns on results shown mid-interview (E2), and the same simulated people talking with three designs that differ only in where decisions live (E3).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Tool-using agents and their evaluation.} Benchmarks such as $\tau$-bench \citep{yao2024taubench,barres2025tau2bench}, ToolSandbox \citep{lu2024toolsandbox}, AgentBench \citep{liu2023agentbench} and MINT \citep{wang2023mint} evaluate agents that converse with simulated users and call tools, and recent work argues that capability gains have barely improved reliability \citep{rabanser2026agentreliability,raj2026consistency}. We borrow the simulated-user setup but score a single high-stakes determination against an exact reference, and ask a design question rather than a capability one.

\paragraph{Asking the right question.} Clarifying-question work ranks or generates questions by expected value \citep{rao2018learning,aliannejadi2019asking,kuhn2022clam,tamkin2022taskambiguity}, elicits preferences \citep{li2023gate,andukuri2024stargate}, and plans questions in games such as 20 Questions \citep{zhang2023twentyq}. Closest to us, Uncertainty of Thoughts \citep{hu2024uot} and BED-LLM \citep{choudhury2025bedllm,hartmann2026amortising} choose questions by expected information gain using the model's own beliefs, and MediQ \citep{li2024mediq} studies asking and abstaining in clinical reasoning. Our setting has an executable answer function, so we compute a question's value exactly by simulating its possible answers, in the tradition of decision-theoretic expert systems \citep{howard1966information,gorry1968sequential,heckerman1992pathfinder} and adaptive testing \citep{weiss1984cat,vanderlinden2000cat}.

\paragraph{Language models over symbolic engines.} MRKL \citep{karpas2022mrkl}, Toolformer \citep{schick2023toolformer}, ReAct \citep{yao2022react}, and program- or solver-aided reasoning \citep{gao2022pal,lyu2023faithfulcot,pan2023logiclm,ye2023satlm,olausson2023linc} hand computation to code, but leave control (which tool, which inputs, when to stop) with the model. We take control away from the model as well.

\paragraph{Benefits, tax and rules as code.} Encoding law as executable rules \citep{merigoux2021catala,mohun2020crackingcode} makes exact determinations possible; we use PolicyEngine-US \citep{policyengineus}. Language models have been used to draft such encodings \citep{kennan2025airulesascode}, to audit eligibility explanations with a solver \citep{sunny2025neurosymbolic}, and to answer caseworkers' questions \citep{gosciak2026socialservices}; applicants report both reduced burden and overtrust \citep{jo2025trustburden}. To our knowledge, no prior work runs the interview itself from an exact engine and compares that design end to end with model-driven alternatives.

\paragraph{User simulation.} We follow goal-conditioned and prompted user simulation \citep{schatzmann2006survey,schatzmann2007agenda,terragni2023incontext,balog2025usersim}, and take seriously its known limits: simulators drift from their goals, leak information, and, when played by assistant models, are unrealistically cooperative \citep{sekulic2024daus,zhu2024reliablesimulator,dou2025simulatorarena,naous2025userlm}.

\paragraph{Roles.} The agent is built to Alexa+'s published requirements for add-ons (an MCP server) and evaluated in our own Alexa+ simulator, since add-on tooling is not public: the MCP server exposes four tools (start a screening, answer, get results, get a plan to apply). The model calls them and speaks; it receives the next question, the reasons it matters, and the results, never a rule to apply.

\paragraph{State.} The household draft travels inside every tool call (nothing is stored). Each fact is \emph{known}, \emph{unknown} or \emph{declined}. Unknown and declined facts are never sent to the engine, which would read them as 0, ``no'', the first county in the state, or ``citizen''. Inputs the programs read that we never ask about are covered by statements true of the engine's default (``no farm income''), checked by a coverage test that traces every input the programs read.

\paragraph{Question selection.} Candidates are the unanswered questions whose applicability conditions hold. Each is evaluated at a low and a realistic high answer, all in one batched simulation. With $F(q)$ the eligibility outcomes that differ between the two answers, $\Delta(q)$ the largest change in monthly benefits and $c(q)$ the question's burden,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{score}(q) = \frac{w\,|F(q)| + \Delta(q)}{c(q)},
\end{equation}
with $w=\flipweight$ (dollars a month), so any eligibility flip outranks any ordinary dollar change. The interview stops when no candidate flips anything and none changes a benefit by $\tau=\$\stopbelow$ a month or more.

\paragraph{Results.} Results are withheld until the essential facts are known. A program is reported as ``if \ldots'' when an unasked or declined answer could still flip it (computed with the same what-ifs) or when it depends on a condition the engine cannot check. A plan for each program (where to apply, what to bring, what happens next) comes from cited, dated cards that may not contain amounts or rules.

\paragraph{Shipped variant.} Voice conversations reward brevity, so the deployed assistant shows results after the essentials and at most \quickquestions\ more questions (the top-scoring one each time), with the rest of the programs an unasked answer could flip reported as ``if \ldots'' (the early-results setting of E2). When the person asks about such programs, the same rule runs on those programs alone. With every program in that focus it is the full interview, run to its stop rule, which is what the experiments below measure.

\section{Evaluation method}
\paragraph{Reference.} For every household, the reference is the engine's answer with every question answered. Agreement with the reference is not real-world accuracy; the engine is tested separately against official figures, in tests that cite USDA, IRS and state sources.
\paragraph{Households.} Tier A: handwritten California and Illinois households, including each failure found by independent reviews. Tier B: generated households, pairwise combinations of inputs and every program's income cutoff $\pm\$1$, restricted to answers that can exist at each age.
\paragraph{Oracle interviews.} An oracle answers each question from the household's truth (and declines what the case declines). This isolates the policy from language.
\paragraph{Simulated conversations.} A model plays the person from the household's facts, in everyday units (``about fourteen fifty a month''). In E3 the person is always played by the other model family from the agent's, since assistant models role-playing users are unrealistically cooperative \citep{naous2025userlm}.
\paragraph{Metric.} A \fq\ is a program shown as ``you qualify'' that the reference says the household does not qualify for. We also report programs missed, hedged results (true eligibility shown only as ``if \ldots''), questions asked and cost.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{E1: Which question next?}
Same households, oracle and question groups; only the choice of the next question differs: value of information (ours), every applicable question in a fixed order, a fixed order with our stop rule, and a random order with our stop rule.

\label{tab:e1}

All four policies reach the reference answer for every household (Table~\ref{tab:e1}), so the comparison is one of burden. Value of information asks markedly fewer questions and takes fewer turns than asking everything. A fixed order with our stop rule asks as much as asking everything for most households: the stop rule fires only when no remaining question can change anything, and a fixed order rarely reaches that point early, because it does not put the questions that settle the result first. A random order is no better. The saving therefore comes from choosing the next question, not from the stop rule alone.

\subsection{E2: What if results are shown early?}
After each question of our interview we compute the results that would be shown, either taking the engine's answer as is (unknowns read as defaults) or as our system reports them.

Read naively, a partial interview is dangerous: before any question, most households would be told they qualify for something they do not, and the share stays near half for the first several turns (Table~\ref{tab:e2}), because the engine reads every unanswered fact as zero or ``no'', and zero income looks eligible for nearly everything. Tracking unknowns removes almost all of these errors; the price is hedging, as most true claims early in the interview are shown as ``if \ldots'', falling as questions are answered. A handful of households are wrong even when tracked. In the ones we examined, eligibility depends on two unanswered facts jointly (an immigration status together with a later answer), while our what-ifs vary each unanswered question, or group of questions asked together, separately, so no single what-if reveals the flip. We report these rather than tune them away; varying pairs of questions would close the gap at a combinatorial cost.

\subsection{E3: Where should decisions live?}
The same simulated people talk with three designs on the same model: \emph{split} (ours), \emph{tool} (the model has the same calculator but chooses the questions, when to stop, and what to report), and \emph{alone} (no tools). All designs end by putting the programs they report on a results screen, which is what we score. We run two model families.
Alexa is Amazon Nova 2 Lite or Claude Haiku 4.5 on Amazon Bedrock, and the person is played by the other one. All 228 conversations, with transcripts and every tool call, are released.
\begin{table}[t]\centering\small
\resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{\input{tables/e3}}
\caption{E3: the same 38 simulated people talking with each design. Scored on the results screen over every conversation; one that never reached results counts as missing every program the household qualifies for.}
\label{tab:e3}
\end{table}
Where decisions live dominates which model is used (Table~\ref{tab:e3}). With the model alone, most conversations end with a \fq, on both models. Giving the model the exact calculator as a tool helps, but a large share of conversations still end with a \fq. With decisions in code, there is at most one \fq\ per model across the 38 conversations; on the same households, an exact paired test separates it from both alternatives on both models. The split is not free. It costs more turns and more tokens (the interview asks until nothing could change the answer). It hedges: some true eligibility is shown as ``if \ldots'' because an answer was declined or a condition cannot be checked. And part of its advantage is a shift from ``you qualify'' to ``maybe'': about a third of its conversations show a program as ``if \ldots'' that the household does not qualify for, far more than the tool design, which almost never hedges. A false ``maybe'' tells the person what it depends on and is far less harmful than a false ``you qualify'', but it is not free either. The split also misses fewer programs than the alternatives on Haiku and far fewer than the tool design on Nova.

Why does a model with an exact calculator still mislead? We re-ran the calculator on the last household the engine accepted from each model before it showed results (Table~\ref{tab:e4}). For every \fq\ of the tool design, on both models, the calculator agreed with the model: the model did not override the calculator; the facts it gave it were missing or wrong (our analysis does not tell these apart). A typical case asks for income and health insurance, never asks about housing or utility costs, and passes a household in which those are absent; the calculator reads them as zero, and the household appears eligible for a utility discount it would not get. This is exactly the silent-default failure our design is built around, and it moves the error from computation into the choice of what to ask, which a calculator tool does not address. Missed programs come mostly from conversations that never showed results, and otherwise from the same cause; the model almost never left out a program the calculator reported.

The model-alone failures concentrate in a few programs: children's health coverage, federal and state tax credits, and state child care and cash aid (Table~\ref{tab:e4b}). Our design's own failures are of a different kind: protocol failures. Its tools refuse malformed calls (a missing unit, an unknown person), and the model usually recovers on the next call. Two Haiku conversations never reached results. In one, the person declined to say whether they rent or own; by design the interview then asks for the rent (``if you rent''), which the person also declined, but the model never recorded that second decline, so results stayed withheld and it kept pressing for housing details until the person left. In the other, the simulated person began narrating results as if it were Alexa and ended the conversation. Earlier runs surfaced two failures we fixed in the server rather than the prompt: a tool that required a unit even for an amount of zero (Nova often omits it), and a ZIP code spanning two counties that broke the interview. The conversations from before the first fix are released alongside the final ones.

\section{Discussion}
\paragraph{What the split buys.} The determination becomes as reliable as the engine and the dictionary of askable facts, and stops depending on the model: the same design gives near-identical safety on two model families, while the alternatives vary widely between them. Errors that remain are visible and fixable in code (a protocol rule, a missing what-if) rather than in prompts. \paragraph{What it costs.} Longer interviews, hedged results when information is missing or declined, and, above all, engineering: an executable answer function, and a dictionary saying, for every input the programs read, how to ask it, when it applies, and what is safe to assume if it is not asked. \paragraph{When it does not apply.} Without an executable answer function (open-ended advice, rules that are not encoded), there is nothing to move the decisions into. The broader lesson for agent reliability is that giving a model a correct tool does not make its use of the tool correct; the decisions around the tool (what to gather, when to stop, how to report uncertainty) need the same scrutiny as the computation.

\section{Limitations}
The reference is a rules engine, not agency decisions. Two states and the programs the engine models, at one point in time. Simulated people are models, not people, and may be more cooperative and consistent than real ones \citep{naous2025userlm}. In our runs, a simulated person once broke role and narrated results, and before we fixed its prompt, another gave in and answered a question it had been told to decline; both are visible in the released transcripts. E3 uses 38 households and two small models, one conversation each: differences of a few conversations are within noise, and only the gaps the paired test reports should be read as findings. Our what-ifs vary each unanswered question (or group asked together) separately, so eligibility that depends on two unknowns jointly can be missed (E2). The interview's follow-up within a declined topic (asking the rent after ``rent or own?'' is declined) depends on the model recording a second decline, which one model failed to do. The design needs an executable answer function and a dictionary of askable facts, which took most of the engineering effort.
